{\Huge\bfseries Online machine learning\\with River and CapyMOA\par}\vspace{.9em}

\textbf{Lab 4}  /  Adam Chgour  /  20 November 2026

This study compares incremental learners and drift responses using ordered data streams. River covers regression, classification and drift detection. CapyMOA provides a second implementation for classification and extends the study to unsupervised clustering and AutoML.

\subsection*{Streams}

\reporttable{streams}{Six public UCI benchmarks and two synthetic streams with known changes.}

Real forecasts use the previous interval's target and sensor readings plus the known calendar of the forecast interval. Only adjacent intervals form forecast pairs. Metro traffic uses the first 12,000 valid pairs after filtering invalid temperatures and keeping the first reading at repeated timestamps. Occupancy is sorted by timestamp and EEG follows recording order. Gas sensor batches are replayed in date order, with seeded shuffling within each batch to remove class grouping.

\subsection*{Evaluation protocol}

The prequential protocol follows Barry (slide 17). Predict with the model at $t-1$, score against the revealed target, and train on row $t$. All rows count, with 0 used when a learner abstains at startup. Whole-stream scores summarize the replay, while 250-row block scores show local changes in performance. Use a fixed order for every replay and seed 42 for data generation and learners with an explicit seed. For linear learners and regression kNN, each feature vector is standardized using only past rows. The supervised trees and Gaussian Naive Bayes use the original numeric features.

\subsection*{Cost and environment}

\labenvironment{} Supervised baseline costs are componentwise medians of three sequential replays with fresh learners. CapyMOA gets a discarded 256-row warm-up per pair. Mean and p95 measure per-row prediction and update latency. Rare slow rows can raise the mean above p95. Wall time includes loop and instrumentation overhead. Imports, loading, feature preparation, learner setup and final metric aggregation are outside the timed loop.

Source code, exact configurations, notebook and raw results are in the \href{https://github.com/adamchgour/Lab-4-Online-Machine-Learning-on-Data-Stream}{GitHub repository}.

\clearpage
\labpart{Part 1 \enspace/\enspace Steps 2--3}
\section{River regression}

\textbf{HT} grows a Hoeffding tree with mean leaves. \textbf{ARF} combines five resampled trees with mean leaves, median aggregation and internal ADWIN-based replacement. Leaves maintain target statistics and use variance reduction to score splits. Both trees use a 100-observation grace period. \textbf{AdaGrad} fits a linear predictor with initial coefficient rate 0.10 and intercept rate 0.05. It standardizes targets online and converts predictions back to the original units. \textbf{kNN} searches a 200-row window for five neighbors and averages their targets.

\reporttable{regression}{Regression errors and cost.}

\reportfigure[1]{regression}{MAE vs. mean cost. Each panel has its own score scale. Latency uses a logarithmic axis.}

AdaGrad had the smallest mean latency on every regression stream. On Seoul bikes, kNN improved MAE from 171.00 to 169.41 rentals per hour at about $60\times$ the mean latency. AdaGrad also had the lowest appliance-energy MAE (33.15 Wh). Metro traffic made a stronger case for the forest. ARF reduced MAE from 520 to 420 vehicles per hour, about 19\%, at $35\times$ the mean latency.

\clearpage
\labpart{Part 1 \enspace/\enspace Step 4}
\section{Regression drift detection}

Friedman shift switches from the first five inputs to the last five and adds 12 to the target at row 5,001. The detectors use absolute HT errors divided by three times the target standard deviation from the first 100 rows and clipped at 1. ADWIN tests an adaptive window mean (\ensuremath{\delta}=0.002), Page--Hinkley accumulates upward deviations (threshold 25, \ensuremath{\delta}=0.005, minimum 30), and KSWIN compares empirical distributions (\ensuremath{\alpha}=0.005, window 100, reference size 30).

From row 101, every detector runs in monitor-only and in reset mode. A reset replaces the tree after predicting the alarm row and trains the fresh tree on that row. Delay is measured from row 5,001 to the first alarm within the next 1,000 rows, including row 5,001.

\reporttable{regressionDrift}{Synthetic results. Pairs show monitor / reset. Scores and latency use reset mode or the unmodified HT baseline.}

\reporttable{regressionReal}{Real-stream counts show monitor/reset alarms and change from baseline under reset (MAE). Alarm positions are in the CSVs. Real streams lack labeled change points.}

\reportfigure[1]{regression_drift}{Synthetic blocks of 250 rows. Vertical line marks the true shift. Alarm ticks denote monitor mode and dots denote reset mode.}

On Friedman shift, KSWIN detected the change after 15 rows, with eight earlier alarms in monitor mode. Resetting with KSWIN cost about $5.4\times$ the baseline mean latency. Page--Hinkley detected the change after 43 rows and cut first-1,000 MAE from 10.79 to 3.55. On Seoul bikes, Page--Hinkley reset twice and improved MAE from 252.32 to 202.46. On Metro traffic, ADWIN and KSWIN resets more than doubled MAE. These replays show why alerting and automatic resetting need separate evaluation.

\clearpage
\labpart{Part 1 \enspace/\enspace Step 5}
\section{River classification}

\textbf{HT} follows the VFDT split rule (Barry, slides 29--32). Every 100 observations at a leaf, it can compare the two best information gains using the Hoeffding bound ($\delta=10^{-5}$) and a tie threshold of 0.05. Leaves predict the majority class. \textbf{ARF} uses five trees with random feature subsets and Poisson(6) resampling. An ADWIN warning starts a background tree. A drift alarm replaces the affected tree with its background tree if one is available (slides 47--50). \textbf{Logistic} fits log loss with AdaGrad at 0.05 and intercept rate 0.05. \textbf{GaussianNB} maintains class priors and per-class Gaussian feature statistics.

\reporttable{classification}{Classification quality and cost.}

\reportfigure[1]{classification}{Accuracy vs. mean cost. Each panel has its own score scale. Latency uses a logarithmic axis.}

The Hoeffding tree led on room occupancy (98.07\%). On EEG, ARF improved accuracy from 90.48\% with logistic regression to 96.78\% at about $8.2\times$ the mean latency. On gas sensors, logistic regression matched ARF's 80.66\% accuracy while processing a row about $13\times$ faster. Macro F1 and kappa were nearly identical for those two gas-sensor models.

\clearpage
\labpart{Part 1 \enspace/\enspace Step 5}
\section{Classification drift detection}

SEA uses a threshold of 8 on $x_1+x_2$ through row 5,000 and 13 thereafter, with 5\% independent label flips. The threshold changes $P(y\mid X)$ while the feature distribution stays fixed (Barry, slide 18). Detectors observe the HT's binary error. ADWIN compares older and recent window means ($\delta=0.002$). DDM sets thresholds using the historical minimum of the error rate plus its standard deviation (slide 19). EDDM uses distances between mistakes and HDDM-A uses Hoeffding bounds. The three binary detectors use River defaults.

From row 101, every detector runs in monitor-only and in reset mode. A reset replaces the tree after predicting the alarm row and trains the fresh tree on that row. Delay is measured from row 5,001 to the first alarm within the next 1,000 rows, including row 5,001.

\reporttable{classificationDrift}{Synthetic results. Pairs show monitor / reset. Scores and latency use reset mode or the unmodified HT baseline.}

\reporttable{classificationReal}{Real-stream counts show monitor/reset alarms and change from baseline under reset (accuracy). Alarm positions are in the CSVs. Real streams lack labeled change points.}

\reportfigure[1]{classification_drift}{Synthetic blocks of 250 rows. Vertical line marks the true shift. Alarm ticks denote monitor mode and dots denote reset mode.}

On SEA, HDDM-A detected the shift after 47 rows and improved accuracy from 77.86\% to 80.72\%. ADWIN improved early recovery but reduced full-stream accuracy to 70.71\%. In the EEG replay, DDM resets raised the standalone tree's accuracy from 58.52\% to 99.78\%. Adjacent labels agree in 99.85\% of pairs in this single recording. The result describes adaptation within that recording under immediate label feedback.

\labpart{Part 2 \enspace/\enspace Steps 7--8}
\section{River versus CapyMOA}

Four pairs use the same ordered inputs, scoring, seed and learner family. HT and ARF align the split settings and majority leaves. ARF also aligns five trees, $\mathrm{round}(\sqrt{d})$ candidate features out of $d$, Poisson(6) bagging, ADWIN drifts (\ensuremath{\delta}=0.001) and warnings (\ensuremath{\delta}=0.01) with one worker. Numeric observers, tie handling and implementation details can still differ.

\reportfigure[1]{cross_library}{Positive values favor CapyMOA on accuracy. Values right of 1 favor CapyMOA on latency. The x-axis is logarithmic.}

\libraryfindings{} Logistic compares AdaGrad in River with a log-loss scikit-learn SGD wrapper in CapyMOA (initial rate 0.05, inverse scaling and L2 by default). This pair differs in both optimizer and wrapper.

\librarymemory{} These are process measurements for finite replays. They include the buffered input, timing arrays and the JVM where applicable. Sampling every 250 rows can miss transient peaks, and this setup does not establish a bound on learner memory for an unending stream.

\labpart{Part 2 \enspace/\enspace Step 9}
\section{Clustering evolving streams}

The real stream consists of the first 6,000 occupancy records, represented by light in units of 100 lux and CO$_2$ in units of 250 ppm. The other stream is a 6,000-row simulation of three 2D Gaussian groups ($\sigma=0.30$) moving at row 3,001. CluStream keeps up to 30 micro-clusters with horizon 750. Its K-means summaries use $k=2$ for the room sensors and $k=3$ for the simulation. DenStream uses a 750 horizon, \ensuremath{\varepsilon}=0.55, \ensuremath{\beta}=0.20, \ensuremath{\mu}=2, 300 initialization points and density decay \ensuremath{\lambda}=0.25. ClusTree uses horizon 750 and height 5, returning its micro-clusters.

Give each clusterer features and a constant dummy class. Before training a 250-row block, assign its points to the nearest center from the previous snapshot. Use occupancy labels or simulated groups only for adjusted Rand index (ARI). Skip blocks before the first nonempty snapshot.

\reporttable{clustering}{ARI is 1 for identical partitions, with 0 representing chance agreement in expectation. Shift ARI is the score for the first block after the shift. Centers are counted at the end. Training latency excludes snapshots.}

\reportfigure[.91]{clustering}{ARI on each new block. The dotted line marks the center shift.}

\reportfigure[.90]{cluster_centers}{Cluster centers at three snapshots of the moving stream. ClusTree reports microsummaries while CluStream and DenStream report macro-clusters.}

On room sensors, CluStream had mean ARI 0.807 and took about 35~\ensuremath{\mu}s per training update. DenStream reached 0.792 at about 100~\ensuremath{\mu}s. ClusTree trained fastest, but its 14 final micro-centers partition the room readings more finely than the two occupancy states. On the moving stream, DenStream reached an ARI of 1.000 over the last four blocks after recovering the three groups.

\clearpage
\labpart{Part 2 \enspace/\enspace Step 10}
\section{Online model selection}

Use the 10,000-row SEA shift again. Compare each selection policy with single Hoeffding Tree (HT) and Hoeffding Adaptive Tree (HAT) baselines at grace period 100. HAT uses ADWIN to detect deteriorating branches and evaluate alternate subtrees (Barry, slide 38).

\textbf{Bandit.} Epsilon-greedy selection with \ensuremath{\varepsilon}=0.15 and burn-in 300 bandit pulls. Candidates are HT and HAT, each with grace periods 50, 100 and 200.

\textbf{Successive halving.} Same six candidates. Remove low-scoring models at each rung with \ensuremath{\eta}=2, a selection budget of 10,000 candidate updates and a minimum of one survivor.

\textbf{AutoClass.} Periodically resample HT and HAT configurations. Start at grace 100 and confidence 0.001 with search ranges 50--200 and $10^{-5}$--$10^{-2}$. Use a 500-row evaluation window, ensemble size 5 and three new configurations per cycle.

\reporttable{automl}{Bonus experiment. One prequential run per method. Post-1k starts at row 5,001.}

\reportfigure[1]{automl}{Accuracy by 250-row block. The dotted line is the true shift. The HT and HAT curves are the fixed-configuration baselines.}

AutoClass had the highest accuracy (90.22\%), 0.88 pp above the fixed HAT, with about $3.5\times$ its wall time. Successive halving nearly matched HAT accuracy with far higher cost and tail latency. The bandit did poorly immediately after drift, when its historical rewards could favor a learner suited to the earlier regime. Fixed HAT is the economical starting point for this stream and candidate space.

\clearpage
\labpart{Conclusion}
\section{Choosing a streaming method}

\textbf{Start with an adaptive linear learner when throughput matters.} River's AdaGrad models had the lowest mean latency on every stream in the two River comparisons. Use the nonlinear alternatives when the score improvement on the target stream justifies the measured latency. For Seoul bikes, kNN's small error improvement came at substantial cost. The traffic results gave a stronger case for paying for ARF.

\textbf{Use selective adaptation when a model must recover quickly.} ARF offers an ensemble trade-off on the known synthetic shifts. For an external alerting policy, Page--Hinkley was effective on the abrupt regression loss increase and HDDM-A gave the best SEA reset result. Where labels arrive frequently and alarm ground truth is unavailable, validate the recovery effect on historical replay before enabling automatic resets.

\textbf{Match the library to the learner and deployment.} River makes it straightforward to insert online preprocessing or a detector in a per-sample pipeline. CapyMOA offers MOA classifiers, a streaming clustering API and automated selection. Its classifier costs depend on the algorithm, JVM and adapter. A Python callback loop and MOA's optimized evaluation path represent different deployment choices.

\textbf{Choose clustering granularity and selection effort deliberately.} CluStream gave the strongest overall agreement when the expected number of groups was known. DenStream recovered the moving groups without a fixed k but depended on the chosen density scale. ClusTree can provide summaries for a separate macro-clustering step. For SEA, the fixed HAT was a strong default before paying for online hyperparameter searches.

\subsection*{Limitations}

The synthetic streams use a fixed seed and Metro traffic uses a prefix. Repeated timings measure execution variability on identical streams. They do not give predictive confidence intervals. The synthetic streams have known change points. The real streams have no labeled change points. Gas sensor batches preserve long-term order but do not provide exact observation timestamps. The observations assume immediate labels and available contemporaneous features. Runtime excludes JVM startup and cannot isolate its contribution to memory use. Results are specific to these versions and settings.
